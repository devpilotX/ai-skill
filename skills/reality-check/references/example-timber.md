# Worked example: starting a timber company

The numbers below are illustrative placeholders, marked `ASSUMPTION:`, written to show the shape of a
correct response. A real run retrieves real figures with search and cites them. Do not reuse these
numbers as facts.

The conversation in this example takes place on 3 March 2025, so a sixty day kill criterion falls on
2 May 2025. A real run uses the actual date of the conversation.

## Phase 1, the consensus answer, now banned

What roughly everyone asking this got back today:

1. Timber is a solid growing market, with construction demand and sustainable materials trending up.
2. Start by writing a business plan and finding suppliers.
3. Differentiate on sustainability certification and quality of service.
4. Look into value-added processing for better margins.
5. Network with local builders and contractors.
6. Start small, reinvest profits, scale gradually.

Every line is true-shaped and useless. None of it touches what decides this business. All six are
blacklisted for the rest of the response.

## Phase 2, the blacklist as the user sees it

```
THE ANSWER EVERYONE ELSE GOT
- Timber is a growing market with sustainability tailwinds.
```

## Phase 3, wider candidates scored for commonness

Wholesale timber trading. COMMON.

Value-added milling and kiln drying. COMMON.

Sustainability-certified premium supply. COMMON.

Inversion: do not buy timber at all, broker other people's loads and never hold inventory. RARE.

Boring: timber logistics, the trucking and scheduling layer everyone hates. SEMI.

Constraint-exploiting: buy standing timber from small landowners who cannot reach a mill. RARE.

Adjacent: sell to pallet and crate makers instead of construction, which is unglamorous, less
cyclical, and pays faster. RARE.

Unwelcome: the interest here may be in owning land and machinery rather than in commodity trading,
which is a different and much worse business. RARE.

Carry the rare and semi-rare forward.

## Phase 5, ground reality

The commodity trap applies in full.

No pricing power, because the market sets the price. Margin comes only from cost position or logistics.
`ASSUMPTION: gross margin on undifferentiated wholesale sits in single digit percent`, which has to be
verified against real trade data before any commitment.

Working capital is the actual business. Pay for a load on delivery, get paid on sixty to ninety day
terms by builders. `ASSUMPTION: 60 receivable days, 0 payable days, stock turned on arrival.` Then
receivables alone, valued at revenue, are 60 days x (50,000 x 12 / 365) of daily revenue, roughly 98,600
of cash locked up for every 50,000 of monthly revenue. Compute it with the `business-model` calculator
rather than by hand. This, and not demand, is the mechanism to test first.

Freight can exceed product value. Bulk low-value goods are a logistics business wearing a product
costume.

Counterparty risk. One builder insolvency on open credit terms can end a thin-margin trader.
Construction insolvency rates move with the cycle, so retrieve current figures.

Cyclicality. Timber prices swing hard with housing starts. Entering at a cycle peak looks like
brilliance for twelve months and then does not.

Load-bearing assumption: access to supply at a cost the incumbent traders cannot match. Without it,
this is buying at market and selling at market while absorbing all the risk in between.

## Phases 4 and 6, why the same idea gets opposite verdicts

Identical sentence from two users. The swap test forces the divergence.

### User A, inherited 40 hectares of woodland, drives a truck, knows local landowners

VERDICT: PIVOT, not into timber trading but into standing-timber acquisition and haulage.

The edge is non-transferable: local landowner relationships plus owned haulage capacity. A landowner
with twenty hectares cannot economically reach a mill, so they hold an asset they cannot liquidate.
Buying standing timber and moving it uses both assets at once and sidesteps the working capital trap,
because the inventory gets bought cheap at source instead of at market price on credit terms.

This is not "start a timber company". It is a specific wedge, available because of who this person is.

Kill criteria: if fewer than three of the first twenty approached landowners sign a standing-sale
agreement by 2 May 2025, the relationship advantage was imagined. Stop. Sunk cost at that point is fuel,
time, and whatever survey fees were paid, `ASSUMPTION:` under 1,000.

Seven-day test: approach five landowners with a written offer on a specific parcel. Signed intent, not
encouragement. Cost is a week of evenings and fuel. Two or more signatures means go; none means the kill
criterion is already in sight.

Hand-off: `business-model` next, to price standing timber against mill gate prices and haulage cost.

### User B, has 30,000 in savings, lives in a city, read that timber is a good market

VERDICT: KILL.

No supply access, no logistics, no industry relationships, no cost advantage, and 30,000 against a
working capital cycle that, on the phase 5 assumption, ties up close to 100,000 at 50,000 of monthly
sales. They would buy at market, sell at
market, and absorb freight, credit, and cycle risk while competing with traders who hold decades of
supplier relationships. Nothing here is a moat, including the enthusiasm.

The seven-day test for a KILL checks the verdict rather than the plan. Ask three timber merchants for a
written trade price and credit terms on one specific load, which costs a few phone calls and no money. If
none offers a price that leaves room for freight and sixty days of unpaid invoices, the KILL stands. If
one does, the verdict moves to NOT ENOUGH INFORMATION and the next fact is whether a builder will buy that
load on shorter terms.

The honest follow-up is not a better timber plan. It is the question of what they actually have access
to that other people do not. If the answer is nothing yet, the first project is acquiring one, and
taking a job in the industry for eighteen months is a real strategy rather than a consolation prize.

Note what was withheld in both cases: no enthusiasm, no claim that this is a great space, no business
plan template. Also no manufactured harshness, since each objection names a mechanism.

## The demonstration

Same input sentence, opposite outputs. The difference came entirely from phase 4 inputs, which are
facts the model did not have until it asked.

Generic advice is what happens when an answer gets generated from the question alone.
